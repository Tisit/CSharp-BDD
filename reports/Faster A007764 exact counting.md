# Wider counters, not cleverer joins, win n=24

Every record computation of A007764, up to the n=27 frontier, used the same algorithm our DenseCounter already implements: noncrossing frontier states ranked by a left/right minimal perfect hash, in-place updates, occupancy-class parallelism, and modular counting with CRT. The record holders did not have a better algorithm. They had 0.5-1.5 TB of RAM and 64-bit residues. The literature's algorithmic shortcuts either do not apply to all-length counting or were tried and dropped. Jensen-style length pruning never fires when paths of every length are counted. Iwashita's meet-in-the-middle join grew faster than the sweep it replaced. And our own rank computation shows the join matrix is full rank for CRT primes. Because our sweep is **instruction-bound, not bandwidth-bound**, the master variable is **bits of CRT information per stored slot**. Sweep cost is nearly flat in counter width, so campaign time scales roughly as answer_bits / bits_per_slot. On 16 GB, RAM caps bits per slot at about 14, which is why "several residues per sweep" gives almost nothing at n=24 unless memory is freed first. Three routes lead to an overnight n=24. The cheapest to evaluate is a census of the deferred array. If only about a third of it is ever live, a 16-bit mode fits in 16 GB and roughly halves the campaign (about 25 h to 13 h), and height-based pruning of dead states plus fused passes can plausibly close the rest. The strategic route is out-of-core streaming keyed on occupancy classes, with wide counters. It is the only way to reach n=25 (about 1 day) or n=26 (a few days) on this desktop. The bottleneck then moves to SSD bandwidth and wear, not the CPU. New territory begins at n=28, which needs about 40x the state storage of n=25 and stays out of desktop reach.

## Four teams, one algorithm, and an indexing trap

The OEIS history shows a single technical lineage. Bousquet-Mélou, Guttmann and Jensen reached n=20 with a Conway–Enting–Guttmann transfer matrix: ten runs modulo 30-bit primes, each run "about 72 hours using 8 processors" of a 1 GHz Alpha ([BGJ 2005](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0506341)). Iwashita, Kawahara and Minato reached n=22 with a counting-only SIMPATH. Their n=22 run took 79 h and 217 GB ([TCS-TR-A-12-60](http://www-alg.ist.hokudai.ac.jp/~thomas/TCSTR/tcstr_12_60/tcstr_12_60.pdf)). Spaans computed n=23-25 in "around a CPU-month on a machine with 4 eight-core CPUs and 1 TB RAM", with 64-bit residues and two full arrays ([Spaans A007764-fast.c](https://github.com/stubbscroll/OEIS/blob/master/A007764-fast.c)). Iwashita's GGCount then produced **n=26: 9 coprime-modulus runs of 18 h each on 30 cores at 480 GB**, and an n=27 run needing "2 days and 1400 gigabytes" per modulus ([TCS-TR-A-13-64](http://www-alg.ist.hokudai.ac.jp/~thomas/TCSTR/tcstr_13_64/tcstr_13_64.pdf)). Guttmann and Jensen (2022) re-implemented the Iwashita scheme up to the same size. Beyond it they produced only *approximate* terms by series extension ([arXiv:2208.06744](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.06744)). The b-file still ends at n=27 ([OEIS A007764](https://oeis.org/A007764)). The last new term is therefore 13 years old, and n=24 through n=27 are re-derivations checked against the `known` table.

Comparing numbers across papers means fixing the index first, because each author counts something different. OEIS n counts vertices per side. GGCount and Guttmann–Jensen count cells, so their n or L equals OEIS n−1: "the listing in the OEIS runs from 1 to 27, which in our notation is L = 0 to 26" ([arXiv:2208.06744](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.06744)). Spaans uses the dual rook grid, and his "n=24 required around 700 GB" refers to OEIS 25 ([Spaans](https://github.com/stubbscroll/OEIS/blob/master/A007764-fast.c)). With w = OEIS n, the main (Motzkin, free-end-as-excess-')') state count is M(w+1) − M(w). GGCount's main plus blocked count is M(w+1) − M(w−1) ([TCS-TR-A-13-64](http://www-alg.ist.hokudai.ac.jp/~thomas/TCSTR/tcstr_13_64/tcstr_13_64.pdf)). Our engine's deferred array has width n−1, so it holds M(w) − M(w−1) slots, **exactly the size of GGCount's blocked set**. So main + deferred = main + blocked, and that sum sets our RAM.

This settles the conflicting n=25 figures in the notes. **16.6e9 is main states only. 22.5e9 is main + deferred, and that is what must be stored.** A 12-bit n=25 array therefore needs 31.4 GiB, not the 24.9 GB that one note derived from main states alone. The brief's "11.1 GB" at n=24 is likewise GiB of main + deferred. A second off-by-one sits in the bit lengths. One note labels 425 bits as n=23 in cell convention, but in OEIS n that is **a(24) ≈ 425 bits (128 digits)**, followed by 463, 502 and 543 bits for n=25-27. That agrees with the brief's "~128 digits" and "~40 runs at 12-bit".

| OEIS n | main states M(n+1)−M(n) | main + deferred | 12-bit | 16-bit | 32-bit | bits of a(n) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 24 | 5.85e9 | 7.91e9 | 11.1 GiB | 14.7 GiB | 29.5 GiB | ~425 |
| 25 | 16.6e9 | 22.5e9 | 31.4 GiB | 41.9 GiB | 83.8 GiB | ~463 |
| 26 | 47.3e9 | 64.0e9 | 89.4 GiB | 119 GiB | 238 GiB | ~502 |
| 27 | 135e9 | 182e9 | 255 GiB | 340 GiB | 679 GiB | ~543 |

Nobody in the lineage used a smaller state space than ours. Spaans' 700 GB matches a kink frontier with w+1 positions and two 64-bit arrays. GGCount's main + blocked form is what we store. Guttmann–Jensen report their state set as the Motzkin main set, M(L+2) − M(L+1) with L = cells ([arXiv:2208.06744](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.06744)), and whether their kink handling needs auxiliary storage comparable to our deferred array was not established. Their code on GitHub was not inspected. That open question matters, because the deferred array is 26% of our memory.

## Instruction-bound sweeps make bits per slot the master variable

Several notes reasoned from the older "bandwidth-bound" profile. The brief's VTune data (Retiring 71%, Memory Bound 2.6%, IPC ≈ 3.1) overturns it, and that changes which levers matter. Cache blocking by occupation pattern will not help wall time. Jensen saw total CPU time fall as per-CPU memory shrank from 3 GB to 50 MB ([Jensen 2003](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0301468)), but that was a cache effect our profile says we lack. The benefit of fused multi-vertex passes, if any, comes from amortizing decode, ranking and loop control, not from moving fewer bytes. Most importantly, per-sweep cost is dominated by per-state instructions that do not depend on counter width. The brief's own numbers show it: at n=19 a 32-bit sweep takes about 6.6-7.8 s against 9.9 s for 12-bit. **The 12-bit mode is slower per sweep and carries 2.7x fewer bits.**

Per unit of CRT information, the ranking is stark. At n=19, 12-bit costs about 0.83 s per bit of residue. A byte-aligned 16-bit mode, assuming it runs near the 32-bit speed, would cost about 0.45 s/bit, and 32-bit about 0.22 s/bit. Campaign time is roughly (answer_bits / bits_per_slot) × sweep_time, with sweep_time nearly constant in width. So the goal is to pack as many bits per slot as RAM allows. The 16-bit per-sweep figure is an inference that the first experiment below should confirm.

The multi-residue literature fits this frame. Clisby and Jensen ran three 62-bit moduli in one pass: "an increase in running time of some 15%" for "an overall decrease in total running time by a factor of about 2.6" ([Clisby & Jensen, arXiv:1111.5877](https://arxiv.org/abs/1111.5877)). Jensen's SAW run did the same with two moduli at +50% memory and +15% time ([Jensen 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6709)). Their gain came from raising bits per state from 62 to 186 while decode cost stayed fixed, which is exactly the mechanism our cost model predicts. Several residues in SIMD lanes are equivalent to one wider counter, as long as the combined width fits a machine word. Below 64 bits, a single wider modulus is simpler and does the same job. **So multi-residue sweeps are not a separate lever at n=24 on 16 GB.** The RAM ceiling of about 14 bits per slot (7.91e9 slots in roughly 13-14 GiB usable) allows at most 14/12 ≈ 1.17x more information per sweep, whether as one 14-bit counter or two 7-bit lanes, and irregular packing would erase that gain. Multi-residue lanes become a real lever in two cases: when RAM stops binding (n ≤ 23 on this box, where 2 × 12-bit fits in 8.4 GiB, or n=24 on a 32-64 GB machine), and inside an out-of-core design, where more than 64 bits per slot become useful.

Pure 8-bit counters are a dead end, because all coprime moduli ≤ 256 together carry only about 363 bits (log2 lcm(1..256)), less than a(24)'s 425. They are also not faster per bit than 12-bit. The narrowest residue width in the published literature is 16-bit, chosen by Jensen and Guttmann "in order to save memory" ([Jensen & Guttmann 1999](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/9905291)). Spaans kept a 16-bit compile option but ran the records at 64-bit ([Spaans](https://github.com/stubbscroll/OEIS/blob/master/A007764-fast.c)).

The run count also depends on what the run is for. For n ≤ 27, every residue can be checked independently against the known value. The 1-2 extra verification moduli are then redundant, which saves about 5%. A few matching residues give strong evidence that the engine is correct. Claiming an independent computation of a(n), however, still needs the full set of moduli with product above the answer: about 36 12-bit runs at n=24 and about 39 at n=25. For a genuinely new term (n=28 and beyond) the full set plus self-healing verification moduli is mandatory. Nothing about a known value reduces the from-scratch cost.

## The frontier is already near-minimal; joins and symmetry do not pay

The brief's first hypothesis, meet in the middle via 180° symmetry, has been tried. GGCount's authors describe exactly this scheme: stop at mid-grid and sum count(A) × (count(B) + count(C)) over matching main states. Its selling point was halving integer size. But "time growth of the matching process against n was larger than that of the basic algorithm in our preliminary experiments", so the records did not use it ([TCS-TR-A-13-64](http://www-alg.ist.hokudai.ac.jp/~thomas/TCSTR/tcstr_13_64/tcstr_13_64.pdf)). Under CRT the memory benefit vanishes entirely, since width is set by the modulus. The mid-row is the peak of the state count, so peak memory does not fall either. The ceiling is a 2x cut in vertex steps, and only if the join is cheap.

It is not cheap. Within each occupancy class of k crossing edges, the join matrix is the one-defect Temperley–Lieb Gram matrix at loop weight 0: entry 1 if gluing the halves makes one path and no loop. This follows from the standard TL definitions ([Di Francesco, hep-th/9612026](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/9612026)). An in-house rank computation (unpublished, Python scripts `tl.py`/`tl2.py` in the session scratchpad) found this matrix **full rank modulo 1e9+7 for k = 5..15**, with determinants containing only the primes 2 and 3. Modulo 2 the rank collapses to 2^((k−1)/2), in line with Cygan–Kratsch–Nederlof's GF(2) matching bases ([arXiv:1211.1506](https://arxiv.org/abs/1211.1506)). For any word-size CRT prime, then, no low-rank factorization across a straight cut exists. The noncrossing frontier is essentially the minimal representation, and only unreachable, dead or symmetry-equivalent states can go.

A naive join sums over loop-free pairs, a meander-like count. We estimate it at about 10^16 operations at n=24, against about 3.5e12 for the whole sweep. The Clisby–Jensen "prescriptive" encoding, where "there is one and only one way of completing" a state ([Clisby & Jensen](https://arxiv.org/abs/1111.5877)), is precisely the Gram-transformed vector, so a descriptive-times-prescriptive join is a plain dot product. Producing the prescriptive half costs another half-sweep, though, which nets zero. A "zipper" that converts descriptive to prescriptive one position at a time, much like Jensen's meander transfer matrix ([Jensen 2000](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0008178)), is the only speculative route to the 2x ceiling. It needs hybrid states that plausibly number 10-100x the frontier, which is fatal under a 16 GB cap. Counting-mod-p lower bounds for general graphs point the same way: Curticapean, Lindzey and Nederlof show that the matchings connectivity rank over Z_p stays exponential ([arXiv:1709.02311](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.02311)). Our verdict is to **park meet-in-the-middle**.

Symmetry offers nothing else of substance. The diagonal reflection fixes the first step only, and "the two cases are merged soon" ([TCS-TR-A-13-64](http://www-alg.ist.hokudai.ac.jp/~thomas/TCSTR/tcstr_13_64/tcstr_13_64.pdf)). Jensen's mirror-state halving needs an up–down symmetric problem ([Jensen 2003](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0301468)), and a corner-to-corner sweep is not one. Diagonal frontiers and planar-separator recursions both cut more edges than a straight row, so their states grow faster.

Pruning is where a modest gain hides, but not the famous kind. Jensen–Guttmann pruning, which cut state growth from about 3^W to about 2^W and saved 200x memory for polygons, rests on a maximum length. States are dropped when Ncur + Nadd > Nmax ([Jensen & Guttmann 1999](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/9905291)). A007764 sums over all lengths, so that bound never triggers, and BGJ used no pruning for these counts ([BGJ 2005](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0506341)). Iwashita–Minato LOOKAHEAD speeds up ZDD construction by 1.1-2.9x, but it applies the zero-suppress rule to node output, not to counting DP ([TCS-TR-A-13-69](http://www-alg.ist.hokudai.ac.jp/~thomas/TCSTR/tcstr_13_69/tcstr_13_69.pdf)). What does carry over is geometric: a state whose arcs are nested d deep needs about d more rows to close them all, so near the end of the sweep high-height states are dead. **Novel inference:** 180° rotation maps the completable set at row r onto the reachable set at row n−1−r. The dead tail at the end should therefore mirror the sparse head at the start. The brief bounds the head at about 8-11% of sweep work (n=19 is 97% nonzero after row 5), so skipping both head and tail could save roughly 15-20% of sweep work, with no peak-memory gain. Nobody has published such a measurement. It is cheap to measure here.

## Occupancy-keyed streaming is the only 16 GB road to n=25

In-RAM n=25 is out of reach at any useful width. Main + deferred at 12-bit is 31.4 GiB, and 4-bit would fit but carries almost no CRT information. The published way past RAM is Jensen's invariant: "any edge not directly involved in the update cannot change from being empty to being occupied and vice versa". This makes each occupancy pattern of the untouched positions an independent subproblem, needing "just two redistributions of the data set each time an extra column is added". Redistribution cost about 15% of CPU time at 1000 processors ([Clisby & Jensen](https://arxiv.org/abs/1111.5877); [Jensen 2003](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0301468)). GGCount uses the same invariant for its 2^(n−2) groups ([TCS-TR-A-13-64](http://www-alg.ist.hokudai.ac.jp/~thomas/TCSTR/tcstr_13_64/tcstr_13_64.pdf)). Conway notes that such "signature invariant groups" can be serialized to SSD even though "SSDs are too slow to be used as swap space for a giant hash map" ([Conway 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.09806)). Korf's delayed duplicate detection is the general external-memory pattern: append, bucket, merge sequentially ([Korf, IJCAI 2003](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/1630659.1630926)). The only working precedent for this sequence, oneesan-gpu, keeps "the authoritative state in mmap files" with per-group undo journals. Its n=26 state files total about 3.79 TiB, and it reports no timings ([oneesan-gpu](https://github.com/Kicky1618/oneesan-gpu)).

Our engine is already halfway there. Its work groups fix the '.' versus paren pattern of the left half, and a far paren flip preserves occupancy. So groups are closed under all right-half updates, and one row splits into a right-half phase streamed by left-occupancy group, then a transpose to right-occupancy keying for the left-half phase. That is Jensen's two redistributions. The straddling update and the deferred array need a design; the rest follows the existing layout.

Back-of-envelope estimates (ours, assuming about 3 GB/s sustained NVMe, 2-3 array passes per row, compute overlapped with I/O):

| Target | Width | Runs | Compute/run | I/O/run | Campaign | SSD writes |
|---|---|---:|---|---|---|---|
| n=24 | 32-bit | ~15 | ~26 min | ~25 min | **~7 h** | ~35 TB |
| n=25 | 12-bit | ~41 | ~1.9 h | ~26 min | ~3.3 days | ~100 TB |
| n=25 | 32-bit | ~16 | ~74 min | ~70 min | **~20 h** | ~100 TB |
| n=26 | 32-bit | ~17 | ~3.5 h | ~3.7 h | ~2.5-3 days | ~330 TB |

At fixed passes per row, total bytes written per campaign are roughly independent of counter width: answer_bits × slots × passes. Wider counters therefore just trade CPU time against the same I/O volume, and the SSD becomes the binding resource. The naive alternative, one full read and write per vertex step, would write about 21 TB per residue at n=25 and wear out a consumer drive within one campaign. Windowing is mandatory. Consumer 1-2 TB NVMe endurance ratings in the hundreds of TBW are general knowledge, not verified here. n=26 would consume a large share of one drive's rated life, and n=27 (about 1 PB written) is not a desktop job. Beyond about 64 bits per slot, lane-packed multi-residue arithmetic becomes the natural way to widen further. That is where the Clisby–Jensen +15% result genuinely applies.

## Experiment ladder, cheapest evaluation first

Following the brief's rule, every experiment starts with state counts or sweep work at n ≤ 20 and uses residue equality against the previous build as the correctness gate. Items marked *sourced* rest on published technique. Items marked *speculative* are our own proposals, not in the literature.

| # | Experiment | Hypothesis | Cheap measurement (n ≤ 20) | Expected gain | Invariant risk | Go / no-go |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E0 | Known-value verification (*sourced logic*) | For n ≤ 27, checking each residue against `known` replaces 1-2 verification runs | None; CRT-plan arithmetic | ~5% at n=24 | None. Does not apply to n ≥ 28 | Adopt for re-derivations only |
| E1 | Width throughput: 12 vs 16 vs 32-bit (*sourced premise, novel framing*) | Sweep time is nearly flat in width, so seconds per CRT bit fall about 2x at 16-bit | Interleaved A/B single sweeps at n=19 and n=21, prime modulus. The 12 vs 32 gap also sets the ceiling for 12-bit packing work | Sets the value of every other item: ~1.8x per bit if 16-bit ≈ 32-bit speed | Low. #2 (byte alignment is simpler at 16-bit) | Go to E2 if 16-bit sweep ≤ 0.75x the 12-bit time |
| E2 | Deferred-array census (*speculative*) | Only a fraction of the M(n)−M(n−1) deferred slots is ever live at once, so a compact deferred store frees ~3 GiB | Instrument the max concurrently nonzero deferred slots per update, and whether they cluster in identifiable sub-blocks | If live ≤ ~35%: 16-bit n=24 fits in ~13.2 GiB, giving ~28 runs instead of ~38, about **25 h → 13 h** | High: #1 (NN consumer reads the deferred slot), #3 (deferred codec width gives lock-freedom) | Go if peak live ≤ 35% and addressable without hashing |
| E3 | Live-state census, head and tail (*novel inference*) | Rotation symmetry makes dead end-of-sweep states mirror the sparse early rows | Forward-reachable ∩ rotated-forward (completable) boolean sets per row at n=14-20, plus the fraction of whole left-half blocks with max height above rows left | 10-20% of sweep work, no memory gain | Medium: #2 if blocks are skipped; codec change if height-bounded ranking | Go if live work < 85% and dead states fall in whole blocks |
| E4 | Fused two-vertex pass in `UpdateBlockRight` (*speculative*) | Instruction-bound, so merging decode, rank and loop overhead of two consecutive right-half updates cuts instructions per state-update | Prototype on right-half pairs only; instructions per state-update and wall time at n=19 | 1.1-1.5x on the fused portion | High: #1 across two positions, deferred chaining | Go if ≥ 15% wall-time gain on the fused updates with residue equality from n=8 up |
| E5 | Multi-residue SIMD lanes (*sourced: Clisby–Jensen +15%/3 moduli*) | A second lane costs ≪ a second sweep | 2 × 12-bit lanes at n=19-21 (fits trivially) | ≈ 1.17x at n=24 on 16 GB (RAM-capped); ~1.7x on 32 GB+ or inside E6 | Low-medium: #2 lane alignment | Go if 2-lane sweep ≤ 1.2x single; otherwise use one wider modulus |
| E6 | Occupancy-keyed out-of-core streaming (*sourced: Jensen, Conway, Korf; our layout mapping speculative*) | Two redistributions per row keep I/O below compute at 32-bit | Simulate at n ≤ 20 with a file-backed array and a small resident budget, counting bytes moved per row; measure the desktop SSD's sustained sequential read and write | n=24 ~7 h at 32-bit; n=25 ~20 h; n=26 ~3 days (projections) | All three invariants, plus group ordering and crash safety (per-group journals as in oneesan-gpu) | Go if ≤ 3 array passes per row and ≥ 80% I/O–compute overlap in simulation |
| E7 | Meet-in-the-middle zipper (*speculative*) | A descriptive-to-prescriptive conversion costs much less than a half-sweep | Count hybrid states for k = 13-17 in Python | ≤ 2x ceiling; likely negative | Separate code path | Default no-go; proceed only if hybrid states ≤ 3x frontier states |

The sequencing follows the numbers. E0 and E1 take an afternoon. E1 decides whether memory work is worth anything, and if 16-bit really runs at near-32-bit speed, E2 becomes the pivotal question. A positive E2 plus E0 brings n=24 to about 13 h on the current box. Adding E3 (about 15%) and E4 (about 20%) projects to 8-10 h, which is overnight. If E2 fails, the in-RAM 12-bit path tops out near 1.9x: E1's packing ceiling of about 1.3x, times E3, E4 and E0. That lands at about 13 h and falls short. The choice is then between E6 and hardware. The brief already notes that 64 GB runs n=24 at 32-bit in about 7 h. A 32 GB upgrade combined with a positive E2 would also put n=25 in RAM at 12-bit, since main-only is 23.2 GiB. E6 is the most expensive item but the only one that reaches n=25 and n=26 on the present hardware.

## Conclusion

The literature changes the question. A007764's records came from the same frontier DP run on machines with 30-90x our RAM, not from a smarter recurrence. The one clever idea tried in 2013, the symmetric midpoint join, stalled on a full-rank gluing matrix that makes it structurally unattractive for CRT counting. So the path forward is engineering against a precise cost model. On an instruction-bound sweep, CRT information per stored slot is what buys time, and every experiment above either frees bytes (deferred census, streaming) or removes instructions (head/tail pruning, fused passes, lane amortization). The deferred-array census is the highest-leverage unknown in the whole plan, and it is also among the cheapest to measure.

The out-of-core design reframes feasibility more than any in-RAM trick. Once the array lives on SSD in occupancy classes, RAM stops setting counter width, total I/O per campaign is fixed by the answer size, and the limit becomes drive endurance. That puts n=25 and n=26 within a desktop's reach, which no published computation attempted. n=28, the first new term, still requires about 5.2e11 slots and hundreds of terabytes of writes per campaign, so it remains a cluster-scale problem unless something beats the ~3^n frontier. The rank evidence suggests nothing will across a straight cut.
